# Title

A dose-dependent damage signal matches stem cell output to tissue need

# Summary

Tissues must replace damaged cells without exhausting their stem cell pools, but how stem cells sense the extent of damage is unclear. Here, we show that factor X, a secreted protein released by dying epithelial cells, sets the number of stem cells that enter the cell cycle after injury. Using lineage tracing and conditional deletion in the mouse intestine, we find that factor X binds receptor Y on quiescent stem cells and activates pathway Z in proportion to the number of dying cells. Loss of factor X or receptor Y prevents stem cell activation and delays repair, whereas excess factor X depletes the stem cell pool. Factor X levels also track with damage in human intestinal organoids. These findings identify a dose-dependent damage signal that matches stem cell output to tissue need and suggest that tuning this signal could improve repair without stem cell loss.

# Keywords

stem cells, tissue repair, damage sensing, intestine, quiescence, cell death

# Introduction

Epithelial tissues lose cells to injury throughout life and must replace them without exhausting the stem cells that sustain them.^1,2^ In the intestine, quiescent stem cells enter the cell cycle after damage and return to quiescence when repair is complete.^3^

Repair scales with injury: mild damage activates few stem cells, and severe damage activates many.^4^ Signals from the niche are known to permit activation,^5,6^ but these signals do not vary with the amount of damage. Whether stem cells measure damage directly, and through which signal, is unknown.

Here, we show that factor X, released by dying epithelial cells, sets how many intestinal stem cells divide after injury. Factor X binds receptor Y on quiescent stem cells and activates pathway Z in proportion to the number of dying cells. This dose-dependent signal explains how repair scales with damage without exhausting the stem cell pool.

# Results

## Dying epithelial cells release factor X in proportion to damage

To find signals that vary with injury, we measured secreted proteins after graded irradiation. Factor X increased with dose (Figure 1A) and localized to dying epithelial cells (Figure 1B). Blocking cell death abolished the increase (Figure 1C; Figure S1A). Thus, factor X reports the number of dying cells.

## Factor X is required for damage-scaled stem cell activation

We next asked whether stem cells need factor X. After conditional deletion of *Fx* in the epithelium, the number of dividing stem cells no longer rose with dose (Figures 2A and 2B), and repair was delayed by 2 days (Figure 2C). Stem cell numbers at baseline were unchanged (Table 1). Factor X is therefore required for damage-scaled activation.

## Factor X activates quiescent stem cells through receptor Y

Recombinant factor X bound receptor Y on sorted quiescent stem cells (Figure 3A) and activated pathway Z in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3B). Deletion of receptor Y blocked both responses (Figure 3C; Figure S2). Sustained excess of factor X depleted the stem cell pool (Figure 3D).

# Discussion

Our results answer how repair scales with injury: stem cells read a signal whose amount is set by the number of dying neighbors. This differs from niche signals that permit activation but carry no information about the extent of damage.^5,6^ A dose-dependent death signal also explains why chronic injury exhausts stem cells: the signal that drives repair, when sustained, removes the pool it acts on.

## Limitations of the study

We deleted factor X in all epithelial cells, so the source cell type is not resolved. Human data come from organoids of four donors and do not address variation between individuals. We show that pathway Z is activated downstream of receptor Y but did not identify the adaptor that links them.
